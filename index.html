Begin by gathering comprehensive visual information, including photographs, approximate measurements, and observable issues. Signs such as leaning, cracking, deteriorating posts, soil erosion at the base, persistent dampness, or recent construction activity above the wall can help distinguish between a straightforward repair and a more complex slope or drainage problem. Accurately measure the retained height from the wall's base to the finished soil level behind it, not just from the visible ground level on the exterior. Loads, Water, and Approval Questions

A retaining wall must manage three primary forces simultaneously: the weight of the soil it supports, the pressure from water moving through that soil, and any additional weight from structures or features situated on the ground above the wall. For instance, a driveway, carport slab, swimming pool shell, or a neighbour's shed can impose significant surcharge loads near the wall's location. Effective water management is paramount; water needs a clearly defined and functional drainage path. Issues with retaining walls are often linked to improperly redirected downpipes, soil erosion caused by water runoff, or depressions forming in the soil above the retained area. A comprehensive drainage plan should incorporate clean aggregate backfill, filter fabric appropriate for the soil type, a properly graded subsoil drainage line, and a lawful, accessible outlet for the collected water. Council approval for retaining walls is not solely determined by height. Brisbane City Council has specific regulations outlining circumstances under which a wall might be exempt from formal building approval. The Queensland Development Code provides deemed-to-satisfy conditions that, if met, can streamline the approval process. However, factors such as surcharge loads, the presence of nearby walls or buildings, boundary positions, easements, development overlays, and protected vegetation can all influence the required approval pathway and design considerations.

Material Choice as a Site Response

Selecting the appropriate material for a retaining wall, whether it be concrete sleepers, timber sleepers, reinforced blockwork, natural sandstone or boulders, or gabion systems, should be a direct response to the specific site challenges. The decision-making process must prioritise load-bearing capacity, the physical footprint of the wall, drainage requirements, and site accessibility over aesthetic preferences. Concrete sleeper systems are often suitable for straightforward replacements, boundary walls, and cut-and-fill projects where considerations for post embedment, site access, and drainage can be effectively managed. Timber sleeper walls are generally appropriate for lower garden walls and terraces, provided that the selection of timber, exposure to moisture, drainage provisions, and ongoing maintenance are explicitly addressed. Reinforced blockwork is a robust option for engineered loads, situations with limited space, and finished residential walls that require integrated footing and drainage solutions. Natural stone and gabion options are heavily dependent on the ground conditions and the ease of access for machinery. Sandstone and boulder walls require careful attention to bearing capacity, block geometry, the availability of suitable machinery, and effective management of surface water runoff. Gabion baskets necessitate thorough foundation preparation, adequate filter separation, and precise specification of the basket type within the project scope.

What to Include in the Written Scope

A well-prepared written scope of works provides sufficient detail for a contractor or designer to understand the project's constraints before visiting the site. A clear scope should document dimensions, observed symptoms, site access limitations, and the presence of nearby structures. It should also specify whether the project involves constructing a new wall, undertaking repairs, replacing an existing structure, or conducting a drainage investigation. When requesting quotes, inquire about how the estimate addresses excavation, spoil removal, footing preparation, backfill materials, filter fabric installation, subsoil drainage systems, the location of the water outlet, surface water management, and site reinstatement. If design or council approval services are required, clarify who will be responsible for preparing the design, who will conduct the site inspections, and what specific documentation will be provided upon completion. For regulated building work exceeding statutory value thresholds, ensure the contractor holds a relevant QBCC licence. It is important to confirm whether the quoted wall height refers to the visible portion or the total retained height. Ask for explicit details on where collected water will discharge and how the outlet can be accessed for future inspection. Verify that any surcharge loads from nearby structures have been accounted for in the design and quote. Before making any deposit payment, conduct a search of the contractor's public QBCC licence.

Common questions

What are the main signs I need a new retaining wall? Common indicators include visible leaning, cracking, bulging, soil erosion at the base, or significant decay of the existing wall material. Changes in drainage patterns above or around the wall can also signal underlying issues.

Do I need council approval for a retaining wall in Brisbane? Approval requirements vary based on factors like wall height, surcharge loads, proximity to boundaries, and specific site conditions or overlays. It's essential to check with Brisbane City Council or consult the Queensland Development Code for specific guidance.

What is the difference between visible height and retained height? Visible height is the portion of the wall seen above ground level. Retained height is the total vertical distance of soil the wall is supporting, measured from the base of the wall's footing to the finished soil level behind it. This is a critical measurement for design and engineering.
